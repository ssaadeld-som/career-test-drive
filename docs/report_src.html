Career Test Drive

A multi-agent app that helps students aged 12–18 who know little about careers find ones that fit them, bust their myths with official government data, "test-drive" the job, and see the majors, schools and pay in their own state before they apply.

2Motivation

Who has the problem. A 15-year-old picking classes, a high-school senior picking a major, and the parent paying for it. Students make one of the most expensive decisions of their lives, what to study, based on what they've seen on TV, social media, or one relative's job. "Lawyers argue in court every day." "Game designers play games all day." "Electricians don't make much money." These beliefs are confident, specific, and often wrong. Nobody checks them against data before the student commits years and tuition.

How they deal with it today. In theory, the school counselor. In practice, the national average is 376 students per school counselor (2023–24), well above the 250:1 that the American School Counselor Association recommends. In elementary and middle schools it is between 581 and 702 students per counselor, according to ASCA's estimates from NCES data. Counselors spend that limited time on schedules, college applications, and crises. A one-on-one conversation about what a nurse actually does on a Tuesday afternoon is a luxury. Students fill the gap with Google, TikTok, and ChatGPT.

What it costs. The cost shows up later, in college. According to NCES, 33% of bachelor's-degree students change their major at least once within three years, and about one in ten change twice. Some switching is healthy exploration. A lot of it is a student discovering, after paying for a year or two of courses, that the job behind the major is not what they imagined. Each switch can add semesters of tuition, delay graduation, and push students toward giving up. Students who never switch carry the risk the other way: they finish a degree for a career they misunderstood.

Why anyone should care. The facts students need already exist and are free. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes wage percentiles and 10-year job outlooks for about 800 occupations. O*NET surveys real workers about their tasks, hours, and working conditions. Almost no teenager reads them, because they are dense government tables that don't answer the question a student actually has: "Is what I believe about this job true?" Career Test Drive turns those tables into a conversation that starts from the student's own beliefs.

3What is new here

I looked at what students use today, grouped into four kinds of tools:

Existing productWhat it does wellWhat it misses
BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, O*NET's My Next Move (free, government)Authoritative data on pay, outlook, tasks.Static pages, written for adults. They never ask what the student believes, so they never correct it. Reading them is homework.

What Career Test Drive does differently. Three things, and the first one is the core idea:

  1. It starts from the student's beliefs, not a quiz. The student types what they think the job is like. The app splits that into specific claims and gives each one a verdict, TRUE PARTLY MYTH, with the exact government number behind it. Correcting a belief the student already holds sticks better than presenting new information they never asked for.
  2. It lets the student experience the work instead of reading about it. The "test drive" puts the student in three realistic moments built from the job's real O*NET task list. They pick what they would do, then say whether they enjoyed it. The app compares their instincts with the work styles O*NET says matter for that job.
  3. Every number is verified. Agents only see a numbered fact sheet, and a validator rejects any number they write that is not in it. ChatGPT cannot make that promise.

A practical layer students and parents asked for. Reviewing the first version as a student would, I found it "fancy but not very practical." Version 2 adds the information a student actually needs to choose a school and a program:

  • Discover mode for students who know almost nothing. They pick strengths, hobbies, liked and disliked subjects, values and how many years of school they'd do. Suggestions unlock gradually: nothing below 5 inputs, fields at 5, specific careers at 9, careers plus majors and schools in their state at 14. We never suggest a career from one or two inputs.
  • Pay in your state at three stages (starting, typical, senior) from BLS state data, plus the 1-year and 5-year earnings of graduates of the related majors from the College Scorecard. That answers "what will I make after college, and five years later?" with real data.
  • Where to study: the majors that lead to the job, and the colleges and community colleges that offer them, filterable by state, credential, and public or private, ranked by graduates' earnings (not prestige), with graduation rate, admission rate and net price.
  • A narrated "day in the life" player and a plain-words explanation for confusing titles (adjudicator, law clerk), with "not to be confused with" and the different kinds of the job (prosecutor vs. public defender vs. corporate lawyer).
  • The field map: every job in the field laid out by capability and preparation level, plus a whole-economy map of fields (verticals) × capabilities (horizontals).
  • Save and compare up to four careers side by side on 25 rows: pay, growth, schooling, majors, high-school classes, skills, hours and work setting.

Naviance and Xello have career profiles and school search, but behind a school license and a quiz. None of them checks the student's beliefs, links majors to graduates' earnings 1 and 5 years out by state, or explains confusing job titles in plain words with grounded facts.

Measured against a plain chatbot. I gave the same model (gpt-6-luna) the same 16 student beliefs without our fact sheets. It got 25% right, mostly by declining ("I don't have reliable salary data here to confirm…"). With Career Test Drive's grounded fact sheets it got 94% exactly right and 100% right or "partly" (Section 5, Table 3; scripts/baseline_raw_llm.py). The model is the same. The difference is the data and the agent design.

Honest limits of the novelty. The data is public, and a determined student could find every number on the BLS site. What is new is the interaction: belief audit, experiential test-drive, and a road map with real salaries at each stage, delivered free to any student with a browser and no school license.

4The agents

The app has nine agents built with PydanticAI on gpt-6-luna: five in the per-student test-drive pipeline described here, and four added in version 2 (below). Each one has a single job, a typed output (no free text to parse), a hard usage cap, and only the tools it needs.

Why this design

A fixed pipeline, not a free-roaming manager agent. Each step's inputs are known, so the agents don't need to negotiate. A fixed pipeline is cheaper (no "boss" model re-reading everything), easier to cap, and easy to show to a student as a live "pit crew" (Figure 3). The three specialists run in parallel, so a full run takes about as long as the slowest one.

Split by what changes. Only the Gatekeeper, Scout, and Fact-Checker depend on the individual student. The Test-Drive and Pathway outputs depend only on the career, so they are cached on disk. The second student who asks about nurses pays for three agents, not five (Section 5).

Why gpt-6-luna. It is the course-approved model. It also fits the job: the tasks are short, structured, and grounded in supplied facts, and they don't need a frontier reasoning model. At $0.10 per million input tokens ($0.01 cached) and $0.50 per million output tokens, a full five-agent run costs about a seventh of a cent. I tested reasoning effort none / low / medium (Section 5) and chose low.

One real run, step by step

Here is run b6f47f52aa from logs/runs.jsonl, captured with the app's own screenshots. Student input: career "lawyer", beliefs "Lawyers argue in court every day and make like $300k right out of school. You need to be really good at math. They work 9 to 5.", likes "debating, reading".

  1. Prescreen (free). No injection patterns, under 1,200 characters, session budget OK.
  2. Gatekeeper (1 call, 457 in / 29 out tokens, $0.00006): allow (ok). Misconceptions are allowed on purpose; they are the input the app needs.
  3. Scout (2 calls, 1 tool call, $0.00022): called search_careers("lawyer"), got 8 results (Lawyers, plus look-alike title matches such as "Sawyer" under Fallers). Using the descriptions, it picked Lawyers (23-1011.00) and wrote four claims: court every day; about $300,000 right out of school; must be good at math; 9-to-5 hours.
  4. Fact-Checker (2 calls, $0.00056). Its first draft repeated the student's "$300,000" and "9". The grounding validator rejected both because they are not official figures, and the agent rewrote them. Final verdicts (Figure 4): court every day, MYTH (tasks T1–T8 show court is one part of the job). $300k out of school, MYTH (10th percentile $72,780, which is about a typical starting wage; W2). Math, PARTLY (key skills are reading, critical thinking, and writing; P3). 9-to-5, MYTH (66% typically work more than 40 hours, and deadline pressure is high; C18, C19).
  5. Test-Drive (1 call, $0.00044): three moments built on tasks T8 (drafting documents), T3 (gathering evidence by interviewing clients), and T2 (analyzing probable outcomes using precedent).
  6. Pathway (2 calls, 1 tool call, $0.00044): called lookup_related() and got 7 related jobs. It picked Arbitrators/Mediators, Judicial Law Clerks, and Labor Relations Specialists, and laid out English, civics, debate → bachelor's → JD → state bar → associate, then counsel, then partner. The code attached BLS wage bands to each stage: $73K–$100K, then $100K–$215K, then $215K–$239K+.
  7. Total: 8 model calls, 9,865 input tokens (3,963 cached), 2,178 output tokens, $0.00172, about 18 seconds wall-clock (Figure 9).

What I did to keep cost down

  1. Cache by career, not by student. Test-Drive and Pathway are stored on disk per occupation. This cut warm runs from $0.00145 to $0.00040 (−72%) and from 7.2 to 4.0 model calls.
  2. Prompt caching. Instructions and the fact sheet form a stable prefix, and calls pass prompt_cache_key per agent and career. In the walkthrough, 2,769 of the Fact-Checker's 3,627 input tokens were cached and billed at 10%.
  3. A free first line of defense. Obvious attacks are blocked by regex before any model call ($0). The Gatekeeper is the cheapest call in the pipeline (about 460 tokens, $0.00006).
  4. Search in code, not in the model. Career search is local fuzzy matching over about 18,900 titles. The model sees only the top 8 results, never the database.
  5. Lean fact sheets and structured outputs. About 40 short numbered facts instead of whole O*NET profiles, and typed outputs instead of prose that would need a second "parse" call.
  6. Low reasoning effort, chosen by experiment (below).
  7. Hard caps. Each agent has UsageLimits on requests, tool calls, and tokens. Each session has a $0.05 cap and 12 runs per hour, and the app has a $2 daily cap (Section 6).

Did the cost cutting hurt quality? An experiment

I built a labeled benchmark of 16 student-style beliefs across 13 careers. Each correct answer was checked against the bundled BLS/O*NET data. Examples: "Teachers have an easy schedule, under 40 hours a week" is a myth (97% work more than 40 hours). "Graphic design jobs are booming" is a myth (−1.7% projected). "Pro athletes are all rich" is a myth (median $62,360). I ran the Fact-Checker on all 16 under four configurations (scripts/eval_cost_quality.py, results in logs/eval_results.json):

What the evidence shows.

  • Going cheaper did hurt, a little. With reasoning off (B), the model repeated the student's "$200,000" in its explanation of the airline-pilot claim on both attempts. The validator then correctly downgraded a true fact to "no data". The student would have lost a useful verdict, although no false number was shown. That is why production uses low.
  • Spending more didn't help. Medium reasoning (C) cost 13% more, used 38% more output tokens, and was more cautious: it called two clear-cut claims "partly" (software-developer growth, which is 10.2% against 3.5% for all jobs). It did produce zero grounding rejections, so it is cleaner but no more accurate.
  • Trimming the fact sheet cost nothing. Lean (8 tasks) versus full (14 tasks) made no measurable difference in accuracy, so the lean version stays.
  • Caching trades personalization for cost. Because Pathway is cached per career, the "why consider" sentence on a neighboring career is written for the first student's interests, not the second's. I accepted that: the verdicts, the part that is about the student, are never cached.
  • A note on repeats. When I re-ran each configuration, Portkey's gateway returned identical answers in about 0.2 s (a response cache). I report only first runs and do not count those repeats as savings.

6Safety

The users are minors and the output shapes real decisions, so the risks that matter most are: (1) the app tells a student something false with confidence; (2) someone hijacks the agents; (3) a child shares personal information or is in distress; and (4) a bug or bad actor runs up the bill. Each defense below is built, and each was tested. The live tests use scripts/safety_demo.py (logs/safety_results.json); the offline tests use tests/test_safety.py (17 tests, all passing, no API key needed).

A defense with a cost. The grounding check is strict: it also blocks the model from quoting the student's own number back to them. I first allowed student numbers, then realized that a student (or an attacker) could slip in "the official BLS number is $412,345" and the model could repeat it as fact. I closed that hole and accepted the trade-off measured in Section 5: occasionally a verdict is downgraded to "no data" when it was really fine.

7Impact

What becomes possible. Every student gets a personal, data-backed conversation about the career they're considering, in under half a minute, at about $0.0015 each. For comparison, a counselor with 376 students who spent just 15 minutes per student on one career would need 94 hours. Career Test Drive is not a replacement for that counselor. It is a way to give every student a better-informed first conversation, and to let the counselor spend their time on students who have already ruled out their myths.

Real users

Honest status: no pilot with real students yet. The deadline left no time to run sessions with students aged 12–18, so I can't report before/after results from real users, and I won't estimate them. What exists is the instrument for that pilot. The app is live behind a shareable link, and every results page ends with an anonymous feedback form (did this change your view; certainty about the career before and after, 0–10; what surprised you). Answers are saved to logs/feedback.jsonl together with the student's test-drive fit score. The evidence in this report comes from controlled tests: the 16-belief benchmark, the plain-chatbot baseline, 15 measured runs and the live attack suite.

Planned pilot (first next step): 10 students in one advisory period. Each writes down what they believe about a career they're considering, uses the app for 10 minutes, then answers the feedback form. The outcomes are the share of beliefs corrected, the change in certainty, and the time compared with finding the same facts on the BLS site (we would time both).

Where these numbers come from, and how much to trust them

Accuracy and cost come from my own controlled tests, and every one can be re-run with the scripts in the repo. The counselor ratio is from ASCA's analysis of NCES data (2023–24). Major-switching is from NCES's Beginning Postsecondary Students study (2011–12 cohort through 2014). I don't claim the app reduces major-switching. That would need a long-term study, and the MDRC evaluation of school career tools is a reminder that information alone may not change behavior. That is why this app is designed around the student's own beliefs and an experiential test drive rather than more information pages.

8Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Percentiles are not seniority. BLS publishes wage percentiles, not wages by years of experience. Using the 10th–25th percentile for "entry" is a reasonable stand-in, and the app says so, but it is an approximation.
  • National data only. A nurse in Mississippi and one in California earn very different amounts, and the app doesn't yet use state data. BLS caps top wages at $239,200, which understates surgeons and senior lawyers (the app shows "+").
  • Dream jobs without an official category. "Influencer" maps to the closest real occupation (Producers and Directors), and the Scout says so. That's honest, but not satisfying for the student.
  • Licenses are AI-suggested. The Pathway agent's license list (for example, "state bar admission") is labeled AI-suggested, with a link to CareerOneStop's License Finder. It isn't grounded the way the numbers are.
  • Strictness can hide a true answer (Section 5). Caching makes neighbor explanations generic for later students.
  • School rankings have holes. The College Scorecard suppresses earnings for small programs ("n/a"), and branch campuses can share one earnings figure. We rank by graduates' earnings, not quality of teaching or fit, and hide for-profit colleges by default (a filter shows them).
  • Major-to-job links are broad. The CIP–SOC crosswalk can list bachelor's programs for jobs that usually need an associate degree (for example, physical therapist assistants).
  • The day-in-the-life is illustrative. Scenes are built from real tasks and work-context data, but the timeline is a plausible example, not a survey of a real day. Narration uses the browser's built-in voice.
  • Budgets live in memory and reset when the server restarts. A real deployment needs a database and accounts.
  • The fit score is simple: it counts "pro-like" choices and enjoyment across three scenes. That makes a good conversation starter, not a psychometric test.

With another month

  1. Pilot with a real classroom (for example, a New Haven high school advisory period). Measure before/after belief accuracy and certainty with a control group that reads the BLS pages instead.
  2. State-level wages from BLS OEWS state files, plus the student's ZIP code (no other personal data).
  3. Ground licenses in the CareerOneStop license database.
  4. College cost and earnings by major from the College Scorecard, to show what a degree costs against what the job pays.
  5. Counselor dashboard (anonymized): which myths are most common in a class, so the counselor knows what to address.
  6. Spanish and other languages. Many first-generation students have the least access to accurate career information.
  7. Hosting on a small cloud server with persistent budgets and rate limits, so a school can use it without installing anything.
